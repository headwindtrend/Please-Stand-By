# Making Suffering Automatic

[Please Stand By](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O9goXtVG-g8) and the moment love turns into proof

At the beginning of [Please Stand By](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O9goXtVG-g8), Wendy speaks about light:

> “Light. It can travel for millions of years before finally reaching its destination… But what if it never arrives? What if it never finds a home?”

This is not a poetic prologue.  
It is the thesis of the film.

Wendy is not afraid of difficulty.  
She is afraid of **never arriving** — of existing in motion, effort, and endurance without ever being allowed to belong.

---

# When home quietly becomes conditional

Audrey, Wendy’s sister, loves her. This is never in question. But love does not stay intact simply by being sincere. It survives only if the frame around it survives.

The film shows us the moment the frame cracks, not through argument, but through an object: a piano.

> Husband: “We could have it shipped.”  
> Audrey: “We can’t afford it.”  
> Husband: “I thought you loved playing.”  
> Audrey: “I liked it when Wendy’s playing it.”

This exchange is devastating because it reveals what has already happened.  
The piano is not just an instrument. It is shared history, shared presence, shared time. Audrey does love Wendy — and yet she decided to move on and leave Wendy behind.

This is how tragedy usually begins: not with cruelty, but with **practical decisions that quietly amputate meaning**.

---

# Coping as currency

When Audrey asks Wendy about her new job, Wendy answers honestly:

> “No, it sucks, but at least I get to make Cinnabons.”

This sentence contains the new rule.

Not fulfillment.  
Not suitability.  
Not dignity.

Just **coping**.

Coping becomes the price of legitimacy.

Wendy is not trying to find work she loves. She is trying to show she can absorb discomfort without collapsing — because that is what a “non-burden” is supposed to do.

---

# “I’m ready to go home now”

The most painful scene in the film is not Wendy’s road trip. It is the conversation where she believes she has finally gathered enough evidence.

> “After I win, I’ll have a hundred thousand dollars, and you won’t have to take care of me anymore.”

This is not ambition.  
It is an offer of release.

Wendy is saying: If I erase myself as a cost, can I come home?

Audrey responds with what sounds like care, but is actually containment:

> “I don’t want to take you out of here when you’re doing so good.”

“Doing so good” does not mean happy.  
It means **manageable**.

Wendy answers with a list — rules, schedules, restrictions:

> “I can’t write when I want to… So I’m ready to go home with you now.”

This is not rebellion.  
It is a plea for **temporal sovereignty** — the right to decide when to think, create, and exist.

When Wendy finally says:

> “I can help you take care of Ruby.”

Audrey responds with certainty:

> Audrey: “You can’t take care of a baby.”  
> Wendy: “How do you know?”  
> Audrey: “Wendy…”

That pause — the inability to answer — is the moment the film exposes the real problem.

Audrey doesn’t know.  
She is forecasting.

And the forecast has already hardened into fate.

---

# Burden as a social inevitability

Later, Wendy encounters an old woman who says:

> “I don’t wanna be anybody’s burden. People have their own lives, and after a while, those lives don’t include you.”

This line does not come from a villain.  
It comes from experience.

It articulates the unspoken law of modern life:

> **Belonging expires when it costs too much.**

Once this law is accepted, no one needs to choose exile.  
Exile happens automatically.

---

# “But I wanted to show you”

At the end, Audrey says what society recognizes as the correct response:

> “I’m so proud of you.”

She praises Wendy’s independence, her execution, her competence.

Wendy replies:

> “But I wanted to show you.”

This is the emotional core of the film.

Wendy was never trying to win approval.  
She was trying to restore **unconditional belonging** through conditional means — an impossible task.

If love is returned because she succeeded, then the love is no longer the same love. It is contingent. It is revocable. It must be maintained.

And Wendy knows this.

---

# The title we never hear spoken

Wendy’s script is titled **The Many and the Few**.

The film never explains it — because it doesn’t have to.

The many are those who fit without explanation.  
The few are those who must justify their presence.

This is not about autism alone.  
It is about how societies decide who belongs by default — and who must earn it.

---

# Light, again

At the end, Wendy returns to light:

> “Where was that tiny speck of light traveling through the universe? … Hoping to get where it belongs?”

The tragedy of [Please Stand By](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O9goXtVG-g8) is not that Wendy travels.  
It is that **arrival is never guaranteed**, no matter how perfectly she follows the rules.

---

# Making suffering less automatic

No one in this film is cruel.  
No one is stupid.  
No one is evil.

That is precisely the point.

The suffering emerges from:
- Fear treated as certainty
- Dependency treated as moral failure
- Love reframed as risk management
- Responsibility privatized instead of shared

This is why the tragedy feels inevitable — and why it isn’t.

If Audrey had blamed the world slightly more, and herself slightly less, Wendy would not need to prove she deserves to come home.

We may not be able to eliminate tragedy.  
But we can interrupt its automation.

We can question when love quietly turns into proof.  
We can refuse to treat care as contamination.  
We can pause before calling exile responsibility.

Sometimes, that pause is enough.

Sometimes, it can keep a light from traveling forever without arrival.
